Google Ads target ROAS: how to set the right target

By Yusuf Aslan · 7 min read · November 2026

Target ROAS in Google Ads is the automated bid strategy where you tell Google how much conversion value you want back, on average, for every unit of ad spend. Google then sets bids in each auction to grow conversion value around that target. The right target sits above the break-even ROAS you get from your profit margin, but not far above the ROAS your account has actually delivered in recent weeks. Below I explain how target ROAS works, when it makes sense to switch it on and how I pick the number, based on Google’s own help pages.

How does target ROAS work?

According to Google’s page on target ROAS bidding, the strategy predicts the value each search could bring and sets max CPC bids so that your average ROAS matches your target. It uses real-time signals such as device, browser, location, time of day and whether the person is on a remarketing list.

The formula is simple: ROAS = conversion value ÷ ad spend × 100%. In Google’s example, if you want 5 in sales for every 1 you spend, your target ROAS is 500%. One detail people miss: with target ROAS, your existing bid adjustments are ignored, except device adjustments set to -100%.

The whole strategy rests on conversion value. If your purchase tag doesn’t pass the order amount, or the same order is counted twice, the algorithm bids against the wrong number. That is why the first job is setting up Google Ads conversion tracking correctly, with sales values.

How many conversions do you need for target ROAS?

Google sets different minimums by campaign type. For the types e-commerce stores use most:

Campaign typeConversion history Google asks for
Search and ShoppingAt least 15 conversions in the last 30 days
DisplayAt least 15 conversions with valid values in the last 30 days (no history needed for new campaigns)
Demand GenAt least 50 conversions in the last 35 days (10 in the last 7), or 100 in 35 days across the account
Video (action campaigns)At least 30 conversions in the last 30 days

Conversions with a value of 0 don’t count. Google also suggests that if you have only just started reporting values, you wait at least 4 weeks or 1–2 conversion cycles, whichever is longer, before switching. In Performance Max, a ROAS target is optional: Google’s Performance Max page says you can assign an optional ROAS or CPA target to your conversion goals. If you are still choosing a campaign type, I compare the options in Performance Max versus Shopping campaigns for e-commerce.

How do you set the right target ROAS?

I put the target between two numbers: break-even ROAS as the floor, and the account’s actual ROAS as the ceiling.

1. Work out your break-even ROAS

Break-even ROAS is 1 divided by your margin, where margin is what is left after product cost, shipping, payment fees and an allowance for returns. If a 1,000 order leaves you 350 after those costs, your margin is 35% and your break-even ROAS is 1 ÷ 0.35 ≈ 2.86, or about 286%. Anything below that loses money, even if it brings sales. I explain why this comes before any budget decision in the 3 numbers to check before raising your ad budget.

2. Check the ROAS you actually get

Google suggests adding the “Conv. value / cost” column and multiplying it by 100 to see historical ROAS. Leave out the last few days: conversions arrive with a delay, so recent days look worse than they are. Google’s own suggested target is also calculated without the most recent days.

3. Set the target between the two

If your actual ROAS is 400% and break-even is 286%, starting a little below 400% is sensible. Set the target far above what you achieve and the campaign enters fewer auctions, so volume drops. Google says the same: a high target can limit the traffic your ads get.

How do you set up target ROAS step by step?

  1. Verify conversion values. Place a test order and check it appears once, with the right amount.
  2. Choose which conversions count. The bid strategy only uses actions included in “Conversions”. Making add-to-cart a primary conversion inflates value.
  3. Change the bid strategy. You can set target ROAS in a new campaign, in an existing campaign’s settings, or as a portfolio strategy in the shared library.
  4. Save it as an experiment. On a large campaign, use the experiment option to compare the change against the original rather than switching outright.
  5. Don’t let budget choke the target. Google says a campaign can spend up to 2× its average daily budget in a day, while the monthly total stays within 30.4× the daily budget.
  6. Wait 1–2 conversion cycles. The bidder reacts to a new target right away, but reaching it can take 1–2 conversion cycles. Don’t touch the target every day.

What should you do if volume drops?

Google’s logic: if you want more conversions, lower the target gradually so the strategy enters more auctions. If you want more conversion value and efficiency, raise it. My own practice is to avoid big jumps and wait at least one conversion cycle after each change.

Google advises against two more things. Bid limits generally restrict optimization and only apply to Search auctions and portfolio strategies. Separate ad group targets can also restrict Smart Bidding. When you review results, compare against the “average target ROAS” metric rather than the number you typed in; it is the traffic-weighted average of your targets over the period.

For short spikes like Black Friday, consider a seasonality adjustment instead of editing the target by hand; I cover it in Google Ads preparation for Black Friday.

Target ROAS or target CPA?

If your products are priced similarly, every sale is worth roughly the same and target CPA may be enough. If basket sizes vary a lot, say a 200 accessory and a 5,000 product in the same account, target ROAS makes more sense because the algorithm can bid higher on searches likely to buy the expensive item. Google’s Smart Bidding guide reports that advertisers who moved from target CPA to target ROAS got on average 14% more conversion value at similar spend (Google data, February 2023). It is an average, so test it in your own account before relying on it.

If certain customer types, devices or locations are worth more to you, Google’s value rules let you adjust the value of those conversions. Get the basic measurement right first.

Frequently asked questions

What is target ROAS in Google Ads?

Target ROAS is an automated bid strategy where you set the average conversion value you want back for each unit of ad spend. Google adjusts bids in each auction to grow conversion value around that target.

How many conversions do I need for target ROAS?

Google asks for at least 15 conversions in the last 30 days for Search and Shopping campaigns, at least 50 in the last 35 days for Demand Gen and at least 30 in the last 30 days for video action campaigns. Conversions with a value of 0 don’t count.

How do I calculate break-even ROAS?

Break-even ROAS is 1 divided by your margin after product cost, shipping, payment fees and returns. With a 35% margin, break-even ROAS is about 286%. Anything lower loses money.

What happens if I raise my target ROAS?

Google says a high target can limit your traffic and volume. Lower the target gradually for more conversions, raise it for more efficiency, and wait 1–2 conversion cycles after each change.

Should I use target ROAS or target CPA?

If your products are priced similarly, target CPA may be enough. If basket sizes vary a lot, target ROAS makes more sense because the algorithm can bid higher on searches likely to bring more valuable sales.
