Meta attribution window: which setting should you use?

By Yusuf Aslan · 6 min read · November 2026

A Meta attribution window is the number of days after someone clicks, engages with or sees your ad during which their purchase is credited to that ad. In 2026 Meta removed the 7-day and 28-day view windows, and click-through attribution now counts link clicks only. For most online stores selling products with a short decision cycle, 7-day click is a sensible starting point, with view and engagement results read as a separate signal. Below I explain what each window counts, what changed this year and how to choose the right setting for your account.

The attribution setting doesn’t just change your report. Meta also uses it to decide which conversions to optimize delivery for. A poorly chosen window can inflate or shrink your numbers and distort the signal the algorithm learns from. If the signal itself is broken, no window will save you, so start by checking your Pixel and Conversions API setup.

What does an attribution window actually decide?

When a purchase happens, Meta looks back: did this person click, engage with or see your ad in the last few days? The window is the length of those “last few days”. With a 7-day click window, someone who clicks and buys five days later is credited to the ad; someone who buys nine days later is not.

Keep two things apart. The attribution setting on the ad set decides which conversions Meta counts when optimizing delivery. The window in your report lets you view the same results through different windows. Changing the report window doesn’t change optimization; you’re just looking at the same data through a different lens.

What changed in Meta attribution in 2026?

Are the 7-day and 28-day view windows gone?

Yes. According to Meta’s developer blog announcement, from January 12, 2026 the 7-day view and 28-day view windows are no longer returned. The windows that remain are 1-day click, 7-day click, 28-day click, 1-day engaged view and 1-day view, which Meta says is consistent with Ads Manager. Some historical breakdowns were also limited: unique-count and hourly breakdowns go back 13 months, frequency breakdowns 6 months.

What changed in click-through attribution?

In March 2026 Meta announced that, for campaigns optimizing for website or in-store conversions, click-through attribution would count link clicks only. Per Meta’s official announcement and Search Engine Land’s summary:

  • Likes, shares and saves used to count as clicks; now click-through covers link clicks only.
  • Conversions from other interactions moved to what was engaged-view attribution, now renamed engage-through attribution.
  • The engaged-view threshold for video ads dropped from 10 seconds to 5 seconds.
  • Billing is unchanged; only reporting shifts.

Meta’s stated goal is to narrow the gap between Ads Manager and third-party tools such as Google Analytics. In practice, some accounts saw click-attributed purchases drop after March while engagement-driven purchases moved to their own line. Keep that break in mind when comparing reports before and after.

What does each window count?

WindowWhat it countsWhen it helps
1-day clickConversions within 24 hours of a link clickImpulse, low-priced products; the most conservative read
7-day clickConversions within 7 days of a link clickA balanced starting point for most online stores
28-day clickConversions within 28 days of a link clickReporting and comparison for expensive, slow-decision products
1-day engaged view (engage-through)Conversions within 1 day of a non-link interaction or watching a video for at least 5 secondsSeeing video and Reels impact separately
1-day viewConversions within 1 day of seeing the ad without clickingExtra context, not a success measure on its own

View-through is the contested part. Someone who saw your ad may have been about to buy anyway, especially in retargeting and loyal-customer audiences, so view-through can flatter results. I don’t ignore it, but I don’t make decisions on it.

What is incremental attribution?

Meta added an incremental attribution option next to standard attribution. As Social Media Today reports, it uses machine learning to predict whether a conversion was caused by an ad and optimizes delivery toward those predicted conversions, whereas standard attribution counts every purchase within the window after a view or click.

It tends to report fewer purchases and Meta shares limited technical detail. My approach: if the account has enough purchase volume, test it in one ad set and compare it against a standard-attribution ad set over the same period using your own order data. Campaign structure matters for clean tests; I cover that in Advantage+ or manual campaign.

Why do Meta and Google Analytics show different sales?

They answer the same question with different rules. Meta credits purchases by people who interacted with its ads within its own window. Google Analytics distributes sales across sessions and channels; per Google’s attribution settings page, the default lookback window in GA4 is 90 days for key events and 30 days for acquisition events like first visit.

Both platforms can claim the same sale, so their totals can exceed your real orders. Your own order system should be the reference point, which is the same principle I use when splitting budget between Meta and Google Ads.

How do you choose the right setting for your account?

  1. Find your decision cycle. Do customers buy the same day or think it over for a few days? Check the time between first visit and purchase in your order data. If it’s short, 7-day click is enough; for very cheap impulse products, 1-day click is worth considering.
  2. Pick one optimization setting and keep it. Choose attribution deliberately when creating ad sets and use it consistently, or your reports won’t be comparable.
  3. Read windows side by side. Look at the same period through 1-day click, 7-day click and 1-day view. If view-through is a very large share, assume some of those sales would have happened anyway.
  4. Compare with your own orders. Divide your store’s total weekly revenue by total ad spend to get a blended efficiency ratio. If Meta’s reported ROAS is always far from it, question the window or the tracking.
  5. Log change dates. The January 12 and March 2026 changes create breaks in reports. Factor them in, or you may switch off a campaign that’s actually working.

How much can the window change ROAS?

A simple, hypothetical example: you spend 10,000 in a week with an average order value of 750. With 7-day click + 1-day view the report shows 40 purchases, which is 30,000 revenue and a 3.0 ROAS. With 7-day click only it shows 30 purchases: 22,500 revenue and a 2.25 ROAS. If your break-even ROAS is 2.5, the campaign looks profitable in the first view and loss-making in the second. Same campaign, two different decisions.

Frequently asked questions

Which attribution window should I use on Meta?

For most online stores selling products with a short decision cycle, 7-day click is a balanced starting point. For very cheap impulse products, 1-day click is worth considering. Read view and engagement results as a separate signal rather than deciding on them alone.

Why is there no 7-day view window on Meta anymore?

Meta removed the 7-day view and 28-day view windows from January 12, 2026. The remaining windows are 1-, 7- and 28-day click, 1-day engaged view and 1-day view.

Why did my Meta-reported sales drop after March 2026?

For campaigns optimizing for website or in-store conversions, Meta now counts only link clicks in click-through attribution. Purchases after likes, shares or saves now appear under engage-through attribution. Billing is unchanged; only reporting shifted.

Why do Meta and Google Analytics show different sales?

They count with different rules. Meta credits purchases by people who interacted with its ads within its own window; GA4 distributes sales across sessions and channels and looks back 90 days by default for key events. Use your own order system as the reference.

Is incremental attribution better than standard attribution?

Incremental attribution uses machine learning to predict whether a conversion was caused by an ad and usually reports fewer purchases. If you have enough purchase volume, test it in one ad set and compare against your own order data.
